Metro — The Booth Agent

You are working on a coding project. You are hungry, or late, and you want to be somewhere else in New York. This page is how a coding agent learned to answer “I'm at X, get me to Y” from inside the session, the way you would once have asked the person in the station booth, by testing a subway MCP one call at a time and turning what it learned into skills.

00

Introduction

The problem is small and daily. I am at my laptop in a coding session, I want to go somewhere in New York, and I do not want to open a maps app, guess which station is closest, walk there and read the sign, and then come back to my project having lost the thread. I want the coding agent to answer the question where I already am: which station, which train, which direction, when, and whether anything is broken tonight.

An MCP server is how a coding agent reaches a service: it exposes tools, in this case the MTA's live subway data, as calls the agent can make. Tools alone do not know how I like to travel or what I mean by “take me to the chai place in DUMBO.” A skill is a repeatable set of instructions the agent follows to close that gap between what I mean and what a tool can do. A composite skill is a skill that calls other skills. Skills are at the heart of Claude Code OS, my collection of them; every skill on this page was added to it with /sync-os, and copies of the five live in the repository linked at the end.

So the order of work was: register the Metro MCP, list its tools, test each one on its own with the expected result written down first, build a small skill for each question the tools left open, test those, and only then build the composite that answers the real question. Everything was recorded in markdown as it happened, including the outputs, the mistakes and the audits, and the record is in the repository linked at the end.

01

The tools and the tests

The Metro MCP is a remote server at metro-mcp.anuragd.me, registered once with claude mcp add, with thirteen tools; seven apply to New York. They list every station with its coordinates and lines, the stations of one line, the live incidents feed, a line's timetable text, the next trains at a station, the stations you can walk to from a station, and a station-name search. Each was tested on its own, one call at a time, with the argument and the expected result written in the tests file before the call (the station search is the one exception, written up after the run), and the result appended with the raw JSON.

The first test returned 496 stations. The second test was drawn from that output, the map you see in every station: every station placed by its coordinates, joined line by line, on a base of borough and county outlines and rotated so Manhattan runs north to south. It is not the MTA's map, but it is unmistakably New York.

The later tests probed the parts a trip planner depends on: the incidents feed, the next-train predictions at one station and then along a whole line, the transfers listed for a station, and the search that turns a name into an id.

02

What we learned

The tools work, and their shape is not the shape of a rider's question. The incidents feed is the whole system, about 180 records at a time, most of them planned-work advisories weeks old, with a handful of live alerts mixed in under MTA's internal line codes. The next-train tool wants a station id; give it a name shared by several stations, such as 34 St-Herald Sq, and the server errors and Claude Code stalls on that error. A line's station list comes back sorted by id, not in travel order, and it carries every station the line serves in any pattern, late-night reroutes and both of a line's branches included. Predictions carry no train identity, so trains have to be threaded by absolute arrival time. And the server answers 403 to Python's default user agent, which is the kind of thing you only learn by calling it from a script.

None of that is a defect in the MCP. It is the distance between a tool built for programs and a question asked by a person, and that distance is exactly what a skill is for.

03

Why skills, and which

Each open question became a skill, and the rule for building them was a taxonomy: a single skill makes one or more tool calls and calls no other skill; a composite skill calls other skills and never re-implements what they already do. Examples are bundled as proof that a skill works, never as its scope; no place or route is hard-coded as a skill's scope; the one hand-written piece of data is the travel order of the three lines that fork, and it only sorts stations the tools return.

  • /check-delay F — a single skill. One incidents call, filtered to the line, live alerts separated from planned work, a one-screen yes or no.
  • /get-route-info F C — a composite. It invokes /check-delay for each line and adds the line's own timetable text from the route tool.
  • /get-train-board York St F — a composite. It invokes /check-delay, then a bundled script fetches the line's stations, sorts them into travel order (by hand for the lines that fork, by geometry for the rest), polls the next trains around the station and draws a board in platform wording: Manhattan-bound, Brooklyn-bound, where the trains are now.
  • /find-nearest-station Eileen's cheesecake — a single skill. It resolves any place, even a garbled name from voice, to a street address, geocodes it, makes one stations call and lists the nearest stations with walk times and lines. It was proven on five places I dictated and then on four more tests, three of them places.
04

The trip problem

With those four in hand the real question could be posed: I am at X and I want to get to Y. The workflow is a guess and a check. The guess, the prior, comes from what the agent already knows about New York: from the candidate stations /find-nearest-station lists at each end it proposes a one-seat ride if a pair shares a line, otherwise one change at a convenient station, otherwise two. Then every piece of that guess is checked against tonight: /check-delay for every line, the line's station list to confirm each station is really on it, the transfers tool to confirm a listed link and its walk time, and /get-train-board at the start, the change and, when in doubt, the end. When a check fails the plan moves to the second route or is re-proposed from the candidates.

Eight trips were run this way, live. From DUMBO to a Hyderabadi place on 9th Avenue the guess was the F with a change to the E; the boards showed the next uptown E sixteen minutes out and the one-seat A from High St about thirteen minutes quicker. From Times Square to a coffee shop on Madison the two-seat way began with the 42 St shuttle; the board showed no shuttle running at all that hour, and the one-seat Q stayed the plan. From the middle of Central Park the guess was the A down Central Park West; the board at 86 St showed downtown A trains not stopping there that night, and the plan moved to the 1 and the 6. At 2:51 in the morning, from a dessert shop on 14th Street to Roosevelt Island, the one-seat F was right and the next train was twenty-six minutes away, which is also an answer.

The shape of the answer took four tries. The first plan was a screen of nested lines and symbols and was unreadable. The second was numbered steps with a sentence of explanation each, which was still too much. The third squeezed everything into three labelled lines, Take, Delay, Or, and was incomprehensible. The fourth, which stayed, is a few short numbered lines, then one line on delays, then one line of alternative if there is one worth taking. Closing the gap between intent and result includes the shape of the result.

05

The skill

/find-metro <from> to <to> is the composite. It takes the two places in any casual form, invokes /find-nearest-station at both ends, keeps every candidate station it prints, proposes the route as a prior, verifies each leg with /check-delay, its own station-list and transfer calls, and /get-train-board, corrects the route when tonight disagrees, and answers in the rider's clock. One or both places missing and it returns null; an unclear place gets one question. What the invoked skills print is read, not shown; the reply is the answer block alone.

Fontainhas → Hyderabadi Zaiqa (12:22 AM, about 33 min)
1. Walk 7 min to High St.
2. A to Manhattan, 12:32 AM. 10 stops to 50 St.
3. Walk 4 min.
Delay on the A: maintenance in Manhattan.
Alternative: F from York St to W 4 St, then E or A uptown to 50 St. About 46 min; E and F delayed too.

After it shipped, three independent reviewer agents audited it against the written spec, the taxonomy and a first-time reader's eye, and their findings were fixed the same hour: no place names inside the rules, no service hours hard-coded, station ids rather than names on every board call, and the reply declared to be the answer block and nothing else. The seven trips it was proven on are bundled with it as examples, byte-identical to the record.
